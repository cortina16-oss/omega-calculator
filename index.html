Omega & Alpha Calculator

Enter item-level survey data, or a covariance or correlation matrix, to get coefficient alpha and omega-hierarchical from a Schmid–Leiman bifactor solution. Everything runs in your browser; your data never leave this page.

Omega-h requires that the number of group factors be specified. You can specify a number of group factors or the tool will do it automatically using parallel analysis.

Most scales today have six or fewer items. A usable bifactor model needs at least six items, two group factors of three items each, and even then the general factor is identified only by constraining its loadings to be equal; with nine or more items (three group factors of three), no constraint is needed. For shorter scales, the tool fits a single factor and checks the residual correlations. If one factor accounts for the correlations, ω is the reliability of the general factor. If it doesn't, α and ω overstate it; for four- or five-item scales, the tool then also gives a conservative estimate of ωh from a bifactor model with equal general-factor loadings, and a panel in the results explains what you need to check before using it.

What you need to do

    Model check: do the factors account for the correlations?

    Bifactor path diagram

    In a scale that is intended to be unidimensional, the loadings on the general factor should be large and the loadings on the group factors small. If this is the case, then omega-h (and alpha) will be large.

    Large loadings on both the general factor and the group factors can produce large alphas but will produce smaller omega-h values. This is the primary advantage of omega-h.

    Item statistics and Schmid–Leiman loadings

    r.drop is the corrected item–total correlation. g is the loading on the general factor, F1…Fk are residualized group-factor loadings, h² communality, u² uniqueness. Loadings of |.20| or more are bold. Red marks a negative r.drop or g loading, which usually means an item needs reverse scoring.

    Group factors

    Items are assigned to the group factor with their largest loading. ω is subscale omega (general + group variance), ωhs is omega-hierarchical-subscale (group-factor variance only, with the general factor partialled out).

    Number of factors, oblique solution and eigenvalues

    Method

    Input. Raw data are reduced to a covariance matrix (listwise deletion). You can instead enter a covariance or correlation matrix directly, which gives identical results to the raw data it came from. Reverse scoring a matrix item flips the sign of its row and column, which is what reverse scoring does to the covariances. Item means are only available from raw data.

    Basis. Covariances gives the reliability of the unit-weighted sum of raw item scores: raw alpha and omega with loadings rescaled by each item's SD. Correlations gives the reliability of the sum of standardized items: standardized alpha and the standardized omega reported by psych::omega. A correlation matrix carries no SDs, so it always uses the correlation basis. The two bases differ only when item variances differ.

    Number of group factors. The tool preselects it with parallel analysis (Horn, 1965) in its common-factor form, as in psych::fa.parallel. It compares the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix, with squared multiple correlations on the diagonal, against the 95th percentile of eigenvalues from 100 random normal data sets of the same size, and keeps the leading factors that beat chance. When parallel analysis finds one factor, the tool fits one factor, and ωh is the one-factor ω; the suggestion is never above one factor per three items. You can override it. Velicer's MAP test (original 1976 and revised 2000) is reported for reference but not used to preselect, because with a strong general factor it usually suggests one factor. In simulated bifactor data with 3 to 5 group factors, the common-factor parallel analysis found the right number every time, while MAP usually returned 1. Parallel analysis needs n. For a matrix without n, the tool uses psych's default of 3 group factors when there are at least nine items; with six to eight items it uses 2 if a single factor leaves a residual correlation of .10 or more, and 1 otherwise; with fewer than six items it uses 1.

    Short-scale estimate (four or five items). When one factor fails the model check on a four- or five-item scale, the tool fits a bifactor model by maximum likelihood in which every item has the same general-factor loading and the items form two group factors (two pairs for four items; three items and two items for five). A two-item group factor needs equal loadings. These constraints make the model identified (df = 3 for four items, 5 for five); without them, no bifactor model with this few items can be estimated. For four items, the tool picks the pairing that fits best; for five, it starts from the two-factor exploratory pattern, and the researcher should confirm or change the grouping. In population tests, the estimate equaled the true ωh when general loadings were equal and was usually too low otherwise: by about .005 to .03 with mild to moderate inequality, about .06 with strong inequality, and by up to .12 when the items with the strongest general loadings formed one group factor, a case the fit test cannot detect. With five items, a wrong grouping can make the estimate too high. The panel also reports the correlation between the two groups’ true scores (each group’s general plus group-factor variance), to help the researcher judge whether the groups are elements of one construct or distinct dimensions; the tool leaves that judgment to the researcher. When one factor fits, the tool reports the one-factor ω instead, because on a unidimensional scale with unequal loadings the equal-loading model would underestimate ωh by about .10 or more.

    Model check. Residual correlations are the observed correlations minus those implied by the reported solution. When n is known and the model has positive degrees of freedom, the tool also reports the maximum-likelihood χ² test of the k-factor model with Bartlett's correction, as in factanal and psych::fa, and its RMSEA. The fit test uses maximum likelihood; the loadings and omegas still come from minimum residual. For a one-factor solution, the tool warns that α and ω overstate the general factor when the test rejects at .05 and the largest residual is at least .05, or, without n, when the largest residual is at least .10. In simulations at n = 300, unidimensional 4- to 6-item scales drew this warning in 10% of samples or fewer, and 4- and 5-item scales with two group factors drew it in every sample.

    Alpha is k/(k − 1) · (1 − Σ σ²ᵢ / σ²ₓ) on the chosen matrix. The confidence interval follows Feldt (1965), using the F distribution with n − 1 and (n − 1)(k − 1) degrees of freedom; it needs n.

    Omega follows the defaults of psych::omega in R: minimum-residual (iterated principal axis) extraction of k factors from the correlation matrix, oblimin rotation, a single higher-order factor extracted from the factor correlations, then the Schmid–Leiman transformation. With two group factors the higher-order loadings are set equal to √r₁₂, as in psych. With one factor, that factor is g, so ωh = (Σλ)² / total variance; it equals ωt when one factor accounts for the correlations.

    ωh = (Σ wᵢgᵢ)² / w′Rw  ·  ωt = 1 − Σ wᵢ²u²ᵢ / w′Rw  ·  ECV = Σ g² / Σ h², where wᵢ is item i's SD on the covariance basis and 1 on the correlation basis. Loadings in the tables are always shown standardized.

    For ordinal items with few categories, polychoric correlations can give somewhat higher values; you can paste a polychoric matrix computed elsewhere as a correlation matrix. Schmid–Leiman results depend on the number of group factors, so check the eigenvalues and the factor pattern before reporting.
