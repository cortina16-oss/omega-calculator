Omega & Alpha Calculator

Enter item-level survey data, or a covariance or correlation matrix, to get coefficient alpha and omega-hierarchical from a Schmid–Leiman bifactor solution. Everything runs in your browser; your data never leave this page.

Omega-h requires that the number of group factors be specified. You can specify a number of group factors or the tool will do it automatically using parallel analysis.

Data

Example data loaded

One row per respondent, one column per item, header row with item names. Excel (.xlsx, .xls, .ods), CSV, TSV or semicolon-separated. Blank, NA, ., -99 and -999 count as missing; incomplete rows are dropped (listwise).

A square matrix, full or lower triangle including the diagonal, from an Excel sheet or separated by commas, tabs, semicolons or spaces. Item names can go in a header row or as row labels. Sample size is optional and only needed for the alpha confidence interval.

Items

Tick the items that make up the scale. Press R to reverse-score an item (score becomes min + max − score, using the observed range).

Results

Item statistics and Schmid–Leiman loadings

r.drop is the corrected item–total correlation. g is the loading on the general factor, F1…Fk are residualized group-factor loadings, h² communality, u² uniqueness. Loadings of |.20| or more are bold. Red marks a negative r.drop or g loading, which usually means an item needs reverse scoring.

Group factors

Items are assigned to the group factor with their largest loading. ω is subscale omega (general + group variance), ωhs is omega-hierarchical-subscale (group-factor variance only, with the general factor partialled out).

Number of factors, oblique solution and eigenvalues

Method

Input. Raw data are reduced to a covariance matrix (listwise deletion). You can instead enter a covariance or correlation matrix directly, which gives identical results to the raw data it came from. Reverse scoring a matrix item flips the sign of its row and column, which is what reverse scoring does to the covariances. Item means are only available from raw data.

Basis. Covariances gives the reliability of the unit-weighted sum of raw item scores: raw alpha and omega with loadings rescaled by each item's SD. Correlations gives the reliability of the sum of standardized items: standardized alpha and the standardized omega reported by psych::omega. A correlation matrix carries no SDs, so it always uses the correlation basis. The two bases differ only when item variances differ.

Number of group factors. The tool preselects it with parallel analysis (Horn, 1965) in its common-factor form, as in psych::fa.parallel. It compares the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix, with squared multiple correlations on the diagonal, against the 95th percentile of eigenvalues from 100 random normal data sets of the same size, and keeps the leading factors that beat chance. The suggestion is never below 2, the minimum for ωh, or above one factor per three items. You can override it. Velicer's MAP test (original 1976 and revised 2000) is reported for reference but not used to preselect, because with a strong general factor it usually suggests one factor. In simulated bifactor data with 3 to 5 group factors, the common-factor parallel analysis found the right number every time, while MAP usually returned 1. Parallel analysis needs n, so for a matrix without n the tool uses psych's default of 3.

Alpha is k/(k − 1) · (1 − Σ σ²ᵢ / σ²ₓ) on the chosen matrix. The confidence interval follows Feldt (1965), using the F distribution with n − 1 and (n − 1)(k − 1) degrees of freedom; it needs n.

Omega follows the defaults of psych::omega in R: minimum-residual (iterated principal axis) extraction of k factors from the correlation matrix, oblimin rotation, a single higher-order factor extracted from the factor correlations, then the Schmid–Leiman transformation. With two group factors the higher-order loadings are set equal to √r₁₂, as in psych.

ωh = (Σ wᵢgᵢ)² / w′Rw  ·  ωt = 1 − Σ wᵢ²u²ᵢ / w′Rw  ·  ECV = Σ g² / Σ h², where wᵢ is item i's SD on the covariance basis and 1 on the correlation basis. Loadings in the tables are always shown standardized.

For ordinal items with few categories, polychoric correlations can give somewhat higher values; you can paste a polychoric matrix computed elsewhere as a correlation matrix. Schmid–Leiman results depend on the number of group factors, so check the eigenvalues and the factor pattern before reporting.
